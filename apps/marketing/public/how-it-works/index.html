Inside FrockBot

Your Bot.
Your rules.
Your frock.

A Bot should fit the person who uses it — its tools, its behaviour, even the interface around the conversation.

FrockBot is a persistent agent in the cloud, with native apps on your devices and plugins that let you dress your Bot however you want.

This page describes the architecture being built, from what ships today through to the target design. The cloud agent loop, six plugin hooks and a small set of core capabilities are available now; the wider platform of device integrations, slots and native clients described here is planned work. Each section and the capability reference label what is available and what is in the plan.

01 / Native where you are

At home on your device,
connected to the cloud.

The conversation belongs to the Bot. The experience belongs on your device.

Conversations live in the cloud, so switching devices means picking up where you left off. FrockBot’s installed apps are built with Flutter and render their interface locally. Native integrations connect that interface to the device’s audio, windows and operating system, and the browser client reaches the same cloud state. Today that means web and macOS in early access; the broader device reach described here is the target.

Web and macOS are available in early access. Android is in development; iPhone and the other device surfaces in the capability reference are planned. A platform appearing in the architecture describes the intended design; availability labels identify which clients are released.

The long-term aim is to use what makes each platform useful: a desktop’s files and windows, a phone’s sensors, a watch’s quick interactions. Those capabilities need explicit support in the client and permission from the person using it.

02 / The cloud keeps the thread

Close the laptop.
The Bot stays.

A Bot is a persistent identity, with its own conversation, notes, instructions and routines.

A conversation should survive a closed tab, a device switch, and a process restart on the server. Each Bot is backed by a Cloudflare Durable Object that holds its state. Each User has a Durable Object too, coordinating account state and installed plugins. Your devices submit commands and render the results; the cloud holds the authoritative state and runs the agent loop.

A Turn is one run of a Bot, started by a message, a schedule or a webhook. It can involve several model requests and tool calls before it finishes or needs your input. Jev reviews the Turn before the first model call, and each complete proposal before text or tools leave.

Input is recorded before the service acknowledges it, so an accepted message survives a dropped connection. Enough of the Turn is also recorded for the system to resume after Cloudflare evicts a process. “Always on” describes availability and durable work, not a CPU that spins continuously.

A little deeper: effects and recovery

Operations that spend money or write to another service need a recorded intent and an idempotency key. Retrying with that key protects against repeating the same operation where the receiving service supports it. Exactly-once execution depends on the external system’s own deduplication guarantees.

03 / A supervisor on every Turn

The Bot talks.
Jev decides.

A fast conversational model stays the Bot’s voice, with a supervisor on the work behind it.

The hosted product does not pick a reasoning level as it goes. The Bot speaks at one fixed setting. Jev reviews each Turn — whether the work is on track, which calls that change something are allowed, and when a specialist should take a slower pass.

Jev is not a plugin. It is built into the hosted service and cannot be switched off. Another deployment can supply a different supervisor without changing the agent loop.

What enforces first

The first behaviour that will enforce is approval of mutating tool calls. A call that changes something outside the conversation is allowed only when review finds your authorisation and a clear match to what you asked for. A rejected call comes back as an ordinary tool result, with a reason the model can see. A read does not need a judgment.

The Bot asks for missing permission in ordinary conversation — text or voice — rather than through a new approval card.

↳

The adapter is in production. The loop is not wired yet. Shipping Jev does not start supervising Turns. Bots still reply. Enforcement begins when review sits between a proposal and a tool.

A little deeper: specialists, policy and later work

Policies are durable. Per-Bot rules outrank account-wide ones; both outrank overridable platform defaults. Locked platform policy always wins. The Bot may propose a policy change through a tool, and that change is reviewed like any other mutation.

Later, Jev can route slower specialist work — planning, research, coding, criticism — and require a Mentor when repeated bad proposals pile up. The fast model keeps authorship after that advice. If supervision is unavailable, the target design stops agent execution rather than running unsupervised; conversation history and settings can still be read.

Plugins may later receive a metered judgment binding for their own questions. That binding cannot approve the plugin’s own effects or bypass Turn supervision.

04 / Dress your Bot

Change what it does
and how it does it.

Plugins can add tools, change the agent’s behaviour, connect services and supply interfaces.

dsh / DeepSeek Harness and Pi showed that the harness itself should be something you can shape. FrockBot applies that principle in a persistent cloud service, with a deliberate boundary between the Bot, its extensions and your devices.

Plugin code runs in a Cloudflare Dynamic Worker—a separate execution environment created on demand, distinct from the Bot’s Durable Object. A User’s installed plugins share one worker. You install a plugin for your account, then choose which Bots run it.

What a plugin can reach

A descriptor declares what the plugin contributes and what access it needs. The Bot calls the plugin worker, and plugins call back through host-provided stubs. The host checks every callback against the serving Bot and the authority admitted for that operation. Agent-loop work is also scoped to its Turn.

Network access is limited to approved hosts, or open network access if you approve it. Because the plugins share a worker, network authority must be understood as shared across that worker’s enabled plugins. Credentials are attached by the host at the network boundary.

Minimal descriptor · current contractJSON 
{
  "id": "research-tools",
  "displayName": "Research tools",
  "version": "1",
  "contractVersion": 7,
  "tools": [],
  "hooks": ["agent/tool-exposure"],
  "grants": ["storage", "http"],
  "network": { "hosts": ["api.example.com"] },
  "contextKeys": ["user", "bot", "session"]
}

A declared host is a request for access. The User still approves each requested permission. This minimal descriptor declares no tools or cards.

The six agent-loop hooks

The host exposes specific moments for plugins to participate. For hooks that return a patch, the next plugin sees the previous plugin’s result, in plugin order. A Turn may repeat model and tool steps.

  1. system-prompt/assembleShape the instructions assembled for the model.
  2. agent/tool-exposureChoose which admitted tools the model sees.
  3. agent/requestTransform the permitted fields of a model request.
  4. tools/pre-executeInspect or veto a tool call before execution.
  5. tools/post-executeTransform the result returned to the agent.
  6. agent/turn-stoppingReceive the notification that the Turn is stopping.

Today, the stopping hook is read-only: plugins observe that the Turn is settling. Request hooks may patch permitted fields but leave the admitted provider, model and credential binding unchanged.

A seventh hook, theme/assemble, shapes the Bot’s look outside a Turn.

Hook excerpt · JavaScriptTool exposure 
export const hooks = {
  "agent/tool-exposure": ({ tools }) =>
    tools.filter((tool) =>
      tool.name !== "email_send"
    ),
};

This hook removes a tool from the model’s view. Tool visibility controls what the model sees; the host enforces authority independently.

Your Bot can write a plugin, too.

You can ask for a new capability and have the Bot write the plugin. The resulting artifact is immutable and identified by its content. The requested access must be approved before activation, within the Bot’s existing authority.

  1. RequestAsk the Bot for a capability.
  2. AuthorThe Bot proposes code.
  3. Check & buildValidate the artifact.
  4. Approve permissionsYou approve what it can reach.
  5. ActivateMount a new generation.
  6. Next TurnThe Bot uses the generation.
  7. Record & revertKeep versions; restore one later.

Versions can be replaced or a previous set of artifacts restored. External effects such as a sent email persist after a code revert. Plugins may also bundle Skills or offer typed services to other plugins; a missing dependency disables the dependent plugin.

What happens when a plugin fails?

The plugin worker has a hard CPU budget. Ordinary plugin failures can be skipped or quarantined so the Turn can continue. The budget belongs to the worker, and a worker-wide failure can affect several plugins. Failures in required, locked plugins can stop the operation. Isolation limits what a failure can affect.

05 / The interface is part of the plugin

Give it a place.
Let it take shape.

Plugins should be able to shape more than the text of a reply.

A card in the conversation, a panel beside your work, a settings section or a whole page could give a capability its own useful interface, backed by the plugin’s stored data. A slot says where something appears; a renderer says how it is drawn. Most of these placements are part of the target design; conversation cards and settings sections are the starting point today.

A2UI is a structured description of an interface. The plugin names components, supplies values and declares actions; the host renders components it already knows, drawn from its own compiled catalogue.

HTML is the intended option for a custom web interface. A contained surface gives the plugin more control over layout. Privileged logic still runs in the worker, and device access still passes through the host.

A2UI card message · current formatJSON 
{
  "version": "v1.0",
  "createSurface": {
    "surfaceId": "research-note",
    "catalogId": "https://frockbot.com/a2ui/catalogs/frock/v1.json",
    "components": [{
      "id": "root",
      "component": "Text",
      "text": "Three sources saved."
    }]
  }
}

A small description becomes a locally rendered card. Read the component schemas ↗

Cards use A2UI; settings sections currently use a smaller host-rendered view format. General A2UI/HTML slots, full pages and the broader placements in the capability reference describe the target platform.

The host retains ownership of the conversation, audit, undo and trusted approval controls. A plugin draws its own interface within those boundaries. Approval identities and the surfaces that let you see and undo a Bot’s work belong to the host. A new native component or operating-system integration still needs to be shipped in the client.

An event on your phone,
an action on your desktop.

A Bot’s work often spans more than one device or service.

The target platform connects those capabilities: a phone event could start a Turn that uses a desktop, and a webhook can already update a conversation card. The plugin combines the pieces; the host decides what is available and allowed. Of the 148 capabilities in the reference below, 10 are available today.

07 / More than a message history

A little continuity
goes a long way.

A useful Bot needs to remember, know how to work and come back at the right time.

Memory

What it knows

Durable notes hold useful facts beyond the immediate conversation. Bot, User and Project scopes let information live at the right level.

Skills

How to do it

A Skill is an instruction directory: a SKILL.md file and its references. Plugins can bundle Skills for the Bots that run them.

Routines

When to return

Schedules and events start new Turns without an open app. Each run belongs to the Bot’s continuing record.

Conversation history records what happened, but not everything in a conversation is worth carrying forward. Memory holds the durable facts, Skills describe how to approach a task, and Routines bring the Bot back at the right time. Because these are distinct pieces, a long chat does not have to carry every instruction and every fact in its context window.

You can give different Bots different jobs, identities, instructions and plugin selections. They share an account’s connections and resources where appropriate, while keeping their own conversations — a small team of Bots without a separate service for each one.

08 / Somewhere to do the work

Your Bot has
a Computer.

A remote browser, filesystem and terminal, with a desktop you can watch or take over.

The current Computer provider uses Fly Sprites: one persistent computer per User, shared by their Bots, with Bot-specific directories and on-demand desktop sessions. Browser logins are shared at the User level. The Workspace gives the work a place to live.

This is where a Bot can browse, use a terminal and work with files. It is also somewhere you can step in when a site needs human attention. Plugins run in their Dynamic Worker and reach Computer capabilities through the host’s approved interface.

A connected desktop app is another client on your own machine. The cloud Computer is a separate environment; your desktop’s filesystem and operating system are reached only through their own client.

09 / Talk naturally. Choose the engine.

Live voice,
durable work.

The interaction can be immediate while the work remains persistent.

Dictation puts spoken words into the composer. Live voice is a real-time conversation: you keep talking while the voice layer hands work to your Bots. The Bot’s durable Turn remains where that work is executed and recorded.

Speak→Live conversation→Bot work→Recorded result

Model choice sits behind an interface too. The provider catalogue contains 28 provider entries, with different authentication and model options. The Turn uses its admitted provider and model; credentials stay in the Worker. Optional model-provider plugins translate requests through host-controlled transport.

Frock AI is the built-in path. The simplest deployment uses the Cloudflare account’s Workers AI inference service directly. Additional providers become available when model keys or an AI Gateway are configured.

The conversational model stays on one reasoning level. Specialist routing belongs to Jev, not to an automatic effort picker.
